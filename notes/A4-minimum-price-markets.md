# A4. Exact minimum prices for fractional assignment markets

L3 reduces exact equilibrium computation to an oracle for componentwise minimum prices in a fractional assignment market. The input gives each buyer type a rational mass and an outside utility, and each good a rational capacity and a reserve. Utility for good \(j\) takes the form \(a_{ij}-d_{ij}p_j\) with \(d_{ij}>0\). All these quantities are binary encoded. A polynomial-time algorithm for finding the minimum prices on these inputs remains open. Given candidate prices, a flow and a graph search can certify minimality. The two Oki–Sato directional auctions have superpolynomial worst-case iteration counts even with unit buyer masses and capacity two. [L3 oracle reduction](L3-prior-partials.md#conditional-exact-reduction-to-a-minimum-price-oracle)

## Direct search and complexity

- **A4a.** Near-factorized slopes admit a polynomial-time exact solver. It repeatedly solves transferable-utility transportation problems with adjusted intercepts and recovers the exact rational prices. The hard auction family below satisfies the slope condition. [Near-factorized solver](A4a-near-factorized-slopes.md)
- **A4b.** A one-good elimination test decides whether a minimum price is at most a given threshold by solving a smaller market and testing a flow. It gives polynomial-time search for bounded elimination depth. An exact tree algorithm also handles interaction graphs that become forests after deleting a fixed number of goods. [Hall search and elimination](A4b-direct-hall-search.md)
- **A4c.** A small rational increase in each capacity makes the enlarged market's only clearing price vector equal the original minimum. This equates the two exact market search problems and, with L3, puts the original game in CLS. [Supply perturbation and local search](A4c-any-clearing-equilibrium.md)
- **A4d.** The market and the original Bayesian game each reduce directly to one P-matrix linear complementarity problem. This gives a UniqueEOPL upper bound for exact search in both models. [Complementarity formulation and proof](A4d-p-matrix-complementarity.md)
- **A4e.** The raw covering objective can select prices that fail unweighted clearance. A slope-based tie-break gives exact descent by reweighted covering LPs, with an exponential bound on iterations. A signed-root transformation gives polynomial time for arbitrary slopes when the intercepts admit common buyer and good offsets. [Covering LPs and unweighted clearance](A4e-covering-lp.md)
- **A4f.** The regularized LCP can have exponential handicap even after arbitrary positive diagonal scaling. Eliminating flows gives a smaller monotone contraction, and exact slope splitting gives another equivalent market. A global polynomial-handicap formulation remains open. [Formulations with bounded handicap](A4f-handicap-formulation.md)
- **A4g.** Candidate price lists can be searched by successive Hall tests, with exact polynomial time at logarithmic incidence cycle rank. The all-ones Lemke path can require superpolynomially many pieces even when the slopes are nearly factorized and the endpoint is directly tractable. [Bounded support search and Lemke lower bound](A4g-budgeted-support-search.md)
- **A4h.** Complete lists give polynomial bit time for bounded-size blocks with at most two articulation goods, and for arbitrarily branching block trees with bounded numbers of original buyers and goods per block. Enumerating small weighted-load lists or contracting after an approximate offset transformation gives two further exact cases. Complete parametric tables can have exponential size. General region search has a polynomial query bound, with the time for choosing the queries unresolved. [Complete candidate generation](A4h-complete-candidate-generation.md)
- **A4h4 and A4h5.** Counting the all-capacity price plateaus is NP-hard even at unit slopes. At the supplied capacities, clipping loads gives a new bounded-block algorithm, and a near-factorized core with single-good branches can use conditional load queries to bypass exponential branch tables. [Table hardness](A4h-table-count-hardness.md) · [Candidates at the supplied capacity](A4h-supplied-capacity.md)

- **A4i.** One strictly signed game with one defender resource returns arbitrary minimum market prices. Together with L4b, this gives polynomial-time Turing equivalence. The unit-market problem is equivalent to exact equilibrium search with uniform priors and equal defender gains. Polynomial-time search with arbitrary affine slopes remains open at unit quantities. [Reverse reduction and unit-market equivalence](A4i-market-to-game.md) · [Open unit-market question](../investigation/A4i1.md)

The polynomial cases in A4a and A4b transfer to games with positive attacker losses. In L3 the price queries keep the loss matrix fixed and use nonnegative reserves. A target can therefore remain viable for a type only if its uncovered utility exceeds that type's best fully covered utility. The interaction graph formed from these viable targets contains every query's interaction graph. Bounded elimination depth and becoming a forest after a fixed number of deletions both pass to subgraphs. Zero defender gains can be handled by A1's exact repair, which preserves the attacker losses. [L3 oracle queries](L3-prior-partials.md#conditional-exact-reduction-to-a-minimum-price-oracle) · [A1 exact repair](A1-threshold-transport.md#4-transfer-an-exact-positive-coefficient-solver-to-weak-inequalities)

## Lower bound for the directional ascending auctions

**Theorem.** For a market with \(n\) goods, \(2n+1\) unit buyers, two units of every good, zero reserves and strictly decreasing affine utilities, the September Oki–Sato ascending auction can require \(n^{\Omega(\log n)}\) iterations. The same bound holds for Algorithm 1 of their October paper applied to the single-seller trading network of §5.1. The explicit market and its network representation use \(O(n^2(\log n)^3)\) input bits. The proof uses the absence of underdemanded sets at intermediate prices and applies to any choices of price directions satisfying the respective algorithms' conditions. [September auction, §3.1 and Proposition 3.11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10563v3#page=8) · [October auction, Algorithm 1, Lemma 4.5 and §5.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40626#page=9)

### The market

Take B3f's \(n\)-target, \(n\)-type game with unit defender gains, uniform type probabilities and attacker utilities

\[
  u_{ij}(p)=a_{ij}-d_{ij}p_j=-p_j+\varepsilon(B_{ij}-C_{ij}p_j).
\]

It has \(|B_{ij}|+|C_{ij}|\le M\) and \(\varepsilon=1/(64nM^2)\). For \(K=n^{\Omega(\log n)}\) disjoint intervals of total coverage in \([1/4,1]\), its best-response graph at any equilibrium admits exactly one perfect assignment, with a distinct assignment for each interval. [B3f construction and forcing proof](B3f-forced-flow-switches.md#why-every-equilibrium-uses-the-prescribed-assignment)

For each type \(i\), introduce two identical unit buyers with utility \(2+u_{ij}(p)\) for good \(j\). Add one buyer whose utility is \(2-p_j\) for every good. Set every outside utility to zero and each supply to two. In the Oki–Sato bundle model, a buyer has a unit-demand valuation, the maximum of her singleton values over the goods present in the bundle, with the empty bundle worth zero. Her payment is the sum of her per-unit charges. These valuations satisfy the substitutes assumptions of both auctions. Removing any paid unit from a demanded bundle must lower its valuation. At most one unit can be essential to the maximum, so every demanded bundle contains at most one positive-price unit, and that singleton is also demanded. [September model, §2.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10563v3#page=5) · [October single-seller model, §5.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40626#page=15)

The bundle model and the fractional assignment model have the same equilibrium prices on these instances. A clearing bundle allocation can keep one demanded option per buyer and discard extra zero-price units. In the reverse direction the transportation constraints at fixed prices admit an integral allocation whenever they admit a fractional one, since supplies and masses are integral. Any remaining zero-price units can be added to demanded bundles within their feasible boxes. A free extra unit preserves optimality by monotonicity of the valuation.

### Why the auction must pass through the forced assignments

Write \(p\) for the good price vector along the auction. For the single-price auction this is the actual price. For the trading-network auction, let \(P_{hj}\) be the transaction price offered by buyer \(h\) for good \(j\), and let \(p_j=\max_h P_{hj}\), the projection used in §5.1. Let \(I(J,p)\) be the original types whose best singleton at uniform good prices \(p\) includes some good in \(J\). At every intermediate price and for every set \(J\) of positive-price goods,

\[
  2|J|\ \le\ 2|I(J,p)|+1. \tag{1}
\]

For the single-price auction, the absence of underdemand means the sum of buyers' maximum demanded quantities in \(J\) is at least \(2|J|\). A core buyer contributes at most one, and can contribute only if her type belongs to \(I(J,p)\). The extra buyer contributes at most one.

For the trading network, take the set of trades \(F(J)=\{(h,j):j\in J,\ P_{hj}=p_j\}\). Every demanded seller bundle sells two units of each good in \(J\) through those maximum-price trades. In the signed demand convention the seller therefore contributes \(-2|J|\) to the upper demand rank of \(F(J)\). If a buyer demands a trade in \(F(J)\), that singleton is demanded at her transaction prices. At the projected prices the singleton costs the same and every alternative costs weakly more, so the singleton is still demanded. The buyers' upper demand ranks on \(F(J)\) are thus bounded by \(2|I(J,p)|+1\). Lemma 4.5 says the sum of the seller's and buyers' upper ranks is at least zero. It applies throughout each price step, giving (1) on the projected path. [October paper, signed demand and Lemma 4.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40626#page=8)

Write \(b=\sum_j p_j\). For \(b\le 1\), all prices lie in \([0,1]\), and every buyer demands at least one real unit because singleton utility is positive. In the network, the same holds at transaction prices, which are bounded above by the corresponding projected prices. The stock is \(2n\), so a clearing equilibrium must have \(b>1\). Both auctions start at zero and their price paths are continuous, including after projection. Each path therefore visits the entire range \([1/4,1]\).

For \(b\) in that range, a price spread larger than \(2\varepsilon M\) would make every core buyer strictly prefer a least-priced good to every most-priced good. A most-priced good is positive and (1) would fail on its singleton. Consequently \(|p_j-b/n|\le 2\varepsilon M\) and every price is positive. Inequality (1) now holds for every goods set and implies \(|I(J,p)|\ge |J|\) by integrality. Hall's theorem gives a perfect assignment of the original types to the goods. Give each assigned pair attack mass \(1/n\). The assignment is a best response in each row, and equal attack totals make the defender indifferent over coverages of budget \(b\). Thus \(p\) is an equilibrium coverage of the B3f game. On every one of B3f's witness intervals the original buyers' best-singleton graph has the prescribed unique perfect assignment.

### Counting price steps

An open review asks whether projecting transaction prices to good prices preserves the required Hall bound along every step of the October auction. The first A4 pass checked three four-good witnesses. A general review can start from the demand-rank argument below and check how often the maximizing trade can change within a step. [Review question](../investigation/2cda0f31-ecad-4bb0-b12e-b040dafa66cd.md) · [Result and exact replay](../records/f9e2da42-7475-4d20-a172-557b2a852ea3.md)

On an affine good-price segment, each pairwise comparison of singleton utilities in one row changes sign at most once in the interior, unless it is identically tied. There are at most \(n\binom{n}{2}\) comparison breakpoints for the original types, giving \(O(n^3)\) open pieces of constant best-singleton graphs. One such piece can visit at most one witness interval in its interior, because a fixed graph can have only one of the prescribed unique perfect assignments. In the trading network, each projected coordinate is a maximum of \(2n+1\) affine functions during an iteration. Splitting at their pairwise crossings gives at most \(O(n^3)\) pieces on which the whole projected vector is affine. Thus one iteration can account for at most \(O(n^3)\) witnesses in the single-price auction and \(O(n^6)\) in the network auction. In a finite run choose a budget in the interior of each witness interval away from the finitely many step endpoints and comparison breakpoint values. The run needs at least \(K/O(n^6)=n^{\Omega(\log n)}\) iterations even when an iteration can pass through several internal demand events.

The doubling of types increases the dense input by a constant factor. The affine payment slopes and singleton values use the bit bounds of B3f, and the extra buyer and the supply constraints use constant-size coefficients. In particular the iteration bound is superpolynomial in the full binary input length with no large supplies. It bounds these price paths. The complexity of computing the minimum prices directly remains open. [B3f bit lengths](B3f-forced-flow-switches.md#binary-input-size-and-the-one-resource-endpoint)

## Certifying a candidate minimum price

For the general fractional input, let \(m_i\) denote buyer mass, \(c_j\) capacity and \(r_j\) reserve. At a candidate price \(p\ge r\), compute the demand sets including each buyer's outside option. One rational feasible-flow test checks whether each buyer can send all her mass to demanded options, with goods capacities \(c_j\) and required sales \(c_j\) for all goods with \(p_j>r_j\).

At a clearing price define \(I(S)=\{i:D_i(p)\cap S\ne\varnothing\}\). Then \(p\) is the componentwise minimum clearing price if and only if, for every nonempty set \(S\) of goods strictly above reserve,

\[
  \sum_{i\in I(S)}m_i>\sum_{j\in S}c_j. \tag{2}
\]

This is the weighted version of the weak underdemand condition. For a compact test, take any flow certifying clearance at \(p\). Draw an arc from a good to each buyer who demands it, and from each buyer to every good or outside option receiving positive mass from that buyer. Search the reversed graph starting from goods at reserve and outside options. Condition (2) holds exactly when the search reaches every good. [Morimoto and Serizawa, Theorem 1 and Corollary 2 for unit buyers](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/150255/1/v10-i2-p445-TE1470.pdf#page=12)

To prove the price test, observe that the inequality in (2) is always weak at a clearing price, since every good in \(S\) is sold out. If an equilibrium \(p'\) lowers any coordinate of \(p\), take \(S=\{j:p'_j<p_j\}\). Every buyer in \(I(S)\) strictly prefers a good in \(S\) to every option outside \(S\) at \(p'\), so all their mass must fit inside \(S\). This forces equality in (2).

Conversely, suppose equality holds for some \(S\). In the current flow all the mass in \(I(S)\) goes to \(S\). Among flows on the tight edges between \(I(S)\) and \(S\) with these row and column totals, maximize the sum of flow times \(\log d_{ij}\). If \(\alpha_i+\beta_j\ge\log d_{ij}\) are optimal transportation dual constraints, put \(v_j=\exp(-\beta_j)>0\). Positive-flow pairs in the optimum maximize \(d_{ij}v_j\) among the old tight edges to \(S\), by complementary slackness. Lower \(p_j\) in \(S\) by \(\delta v_j\) for sufficiently small \(\delta>0\). Their prices stay above reserve. Among old best options, the new flow uses the largest utility gains, and options with a strict old utility gap cannot overtake at a sufficiently small step. Buyers outside \(I(S)\) keep their previous flow by the same slack argument. This gives a clearing price strictly below \(p\) on \(S\).

For the graph test, equality for \(S\) means all the positive flow of buyers reached from \(S\) stays in \(S\). Thus no good in \(S\) can reach a good at reserve or an outside option. Conversely, if some goods cannot reach such a vertex, the set of all those goods lies above reserve, and all flow from their neighboring buyers stays in that set. This gives equality in (2). The verifier uses only rational arithmetic and the given capacities, even though the existence proof for a local descent direction uses logarithms.
