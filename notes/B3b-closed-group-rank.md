# B3b. Polynomial progress inside a closed group of targets

The fixed-order Catcher–Evader continuation computes an exact equilibrium in polynomial bit time when \(P,G,D\) are fixed. Here \(P\) counts distinct positive priors, \(G\) counts distinct positive inverse defender gains, and \(D\) counts distinct positive attacker loss slopes after removing arbitrary row and target factors. Types may overlap on every target, and the uncovered attack payoffs are arbitrary. In particular, uniform priors, equal defender gains and two arbitrary positive slope values suffice. The proof below also allows arbitrarily many rational slope values when their alternating products around cycles generate a group of bounded multiplicative rank. [Conitzer’s problem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.08796) · [Li, Conitzer and Korzhyk, Algorithms 1–4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.01896#page=7) · [Exact implementation in L2](L2-exact-arithmetic-audit.md)

## The bound

Work first with \(k\ge1\) positive-prior types, \(n\ge1\) targets, \(m=n+k\), positive rational attacker losses \(d_{it}\) and defender gains \(\beta_t\), and input length \(L\). Write \(q_{it}\) for attack mass, \(p_i=\sum_tq_{it}\), \(z_t=\sum_iq_{it}\), \(x_t\) for coverage, and \(v_i=\max_t(a_{it}-d_{it}x_t)\). An edge is active when it attains \(v_i\). Before budget completion set \(\lambda=\max_{x_t<1}\beta_t z_t\) and \(B=\{t:x_t<1,\ \beta_tz_t=\lambda\}\). Algorithm 2 minimizes \(\sum q_{it}\log d_{it}\) on active edges at the current margins. Its uncapped residual graph has the forward arc of every active edge and the reverse arc of every positive entry, with opposite costs. Algorithm 3 takes the first target in a fixed order whose reachable targets all belong to \(B\). Algorithm 4 follows a failure and decreases \(\lambda\). L2 gives exact event and flow rules, including ties and endpoint cases. [L2, state and exact steps](L2-exact-arithmetic-audit.md#state-and-exact-steps)

Let \(k_j\) count rows having the \(j\)-th prior value, and \(n_l\) count targets having the \(l\)-th inverse gain. Suppose that on all viable edges
\[
d_{it}=r_i h_t\ell_{it},\qquad r_i,h_t>0,\qquad
|\{\ell_{it}\}|\le D .
\]
An edge is viable if \(a_{it}\ge\max_s(a_{is}-d_{is})\). Every active edge is viable. This test uses payoffs over the whole coverage cube. [B1, viable choices](B1-classify-run-lengths.md#counting-the-signatures-two-ways)

Define
\[
\begin{aligned}
H&=1+\left[\prod_{j=1}^P(k_j+1)-1\right]
          \left[\prod_{l=1}^G(n_l+1)-1\right],\\
C&=(2m^2+1)^{(D-1)(P+G)},\qquad Q=(2m+1)^{D-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Then the number of outer iterations, counting both successful and failed calls to Algorithm 3, is at most
\[
\boxed{(H+2n)\bigl\{1+nm\,C[1+m(Q-1)]\bigr\}.} \tag{1}
\]
The count does not depend on the magnitudes of the payoffs or on denominators. With uniform priors and equal gains one can replace \(C\) by \((1+kn)^{D-1}\). Thus the two-slope example above has \(O((n+k)^8)\) outer iterations. Each call uses polynomial bit time along a polynomial-length run. The algorithm works on the original rational slopes and needs no factors or labels as input.

## Thresholds and changes of seed

At a successful input with seed \(b\), write \(U\) for its reachable rows and \(S\) for its reachable targets. Each active neighbor of a row of \(U\) lies in \(S\). Every positive entry into \(S\) comes from \(U\) by its reverse arc. Therefore \(p(U)=z(S)\), and \(b\in S\subseteq B\) gives
\[
\lambda=\frac{p(U)}{\sum_{t\in S}1/\beta_t}. \tag{2}
\]
This is the tight cut in the weighted Hall formula of L2. Its numerator is a subset sum of priors, and its positive denominator is a subset sum of inverse gains. Counting multiplicity vectors gives at most \(H\) distinct successful thresholds, including zero. The equality uses the selected seed’s own group. [L2, threshold at a successful input](L2-exact-arithmetic-audit.md#non-repetition-with-exact-flow-ties)

The threshold is globally nonincreasing. Coverage increases keep \(z\) fixed and may remove a maximizer by saturation. Every Algorithm 4 strictly decreases \(\lambda\), retains the old members of \(B\), and can add targets. A member leaves \(B\) only at saturation, which is permanent. Consequently \(B\) changes at most \(2n\) times. In the sequence of successful inputs, split on a change of \(\lambda\) or \(B\). This gives at most \(H+2n\) blocks. Between two adjacent successes in the same block there was no failure, since a failure strictly drops \(\lambda\). Their entire vector \(z\) agrees, including on targets outside \(B\). Of the failed calls, at most \(2n\) change \(B\). Each other failure is followed by a success, by the maximal decrease rule, and the thresholds of these following successes are distinct by strict decrease. Thus there are at most \(H+2n\) failed calls. [Li et al., Lemmas 5–7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.01896#page=9) · [L2, exact maximum-decrease LP](L2-exact-arithmetic-audit.md#state-and-exact-steps)

Consider adjacent successes in one block. Write \(\delta_u=\operatorname{dist}_b(u)\) in the first optimal residual graph and \(\gamma_u=\exp(-\delta_u)\), with rate zero at infinity. The coverage increase keeps the old flow \(q\) feasible on the new active graph. A lost forward arc \(i\to t\) has \(d_{it}\gamma_t>\gamma_i\), hence its endpoint \(t\) was reachable. If \(i\) was reachable, the arc had strictly positive reduced cost \(\log d_{it}+\delta_i-\delta_t\). Every old shortest path from \(b\) survives using \(q\), since all its arcs are tight and its reverse arcs have positive flow. An old path from an ineligible seed to a target outside \(B\) avoids the entire reachable set of \(b\), since a path entering it would stay inside that closed set. Such a path survives every deletion.

At fixed active graph and margins, residual reachability is the same for any two feasible flows \(q,h\). A reverse arc present only at \(h\) has a positive forward difference on its edge in \(h-q\). This difference is a circulation and decomposes into positive multiples of directed residual cycles at \(q\). Removing the forward edge of such a cycle gives a replacement path for the new reverse arc. Interchange the two flows for the reverse inclusion. When both flows are true minimizers, every cycle in that decomposition has zero cost, by optimality and equality of total costs. The replacement preserves shortest distances as well. [L2, optimal-flow ties](L2-exact-arithmetic-audit.md#non-repetition-with-exact-flow-ties)

Thus ineligible seeds stay ineligible in the block and the selected seed can change at most \(n-1\) times. As long as the **selected** seed stays \(b\), its whole reachable vertex set can only grow, even when the minimum flow cost falls. The surviving old shortest paths and unweighted reoptimization invariance prove this latter assertion. There are at most \(m\) consecutive constant-set intervals per selected seed.

## The local cost and distance counts

Fix such an interval, with row set \(U\) and target set \(S\). In every active graph in it all edges out of \(U\) end in \(S\). Because \(p(U)=z(S)\), **every feasible flow** at these margins assigns zero to cross edges and splits into a transportation flow on \(U\times S\) and one on \(U^c\times S^c\). Conversely any feasible flows on the two induced graphs concatenate. Outside \(S\) all coverages are fixed, and outside \(U\) all row values are fixed, since their rates are zero. Therefore the active edges in \(U^c\times S^c\) and their optimal cost stay fixed. The load of an outside target may be any previous rational value. Only the inside margins, \(p_i\) and \(\lambda/\beta_t\), enter the varying optimum.

Normalize one of the labels to 1 by absorbing its value into the row factors. Write \(\phi(i)=-\log r_i\) for a row and \(\phi(t)=\log h_t\) for a target. Then \(\log d_{it}=\phi(t)-\phi(i)+\log\ell_{it}\). The first two terms contribute the fixed margin expression \(\sum_{t\in S}z_t\phi(t)-\sum_{i\in U}p_i\phi(i)\). A local cost minimizer exists at a transportation vertex. Its positive support is a forest, since a positive cycle would admit a two-sided alternating perturbation. Removing any positive edge \(e\) cuts its tree. Summing the row balances minus the target balances on the side containing the row endpoint gives
\[
q_e=\sum_{i\text{ on the cut side}}p_i-
     \sum_{t\text{ on the cut side}}\lambda/\beta_t
    =\sum_{j=1}^{P+G}c_{ej}M_j. \tag{3}
\]
Here \(M_j\) ranges over the \(P\) distinct priors and the \(G\) values \(\lambda/\beta_t\) indexed by inverse-gain class, and \(|c_{ej}|\le m\). There are at most \(m-1\) positive edges. For each of the \(D-1\) remaining labels and each \(M_j\), the coefficient of \(M_j\log\ell\) in the local cost is an integer in \([-m^2,m^2]\). This gives \(C\) possible optimum values. Equalities among these real numbers can only decrease the count. In the uniform case, \(p_i=1/k\) and \(z_t=|U|/(k|S|)\) in the group. Scaling by \(k|S|\) makes all vertex entries integral by (3), and the total scaled flow is \(|U||S|\le kn\). Each label’s scaled mass is an integer between zero and \(kn\), giving the stated smaller \(C\).

The old flow remains feasible after each event, so the total optimum is nonincreasing. At fixed \(U,S\) the same holds for the local optimum. At equal local cost the old flow is still globally optimal on the new graph. Old shortest paths survive, so all distances weakly decrease after reoptimization. Between adjacent inputs still inside this interval the step hit neither the budget nor a cap, since the budget ends the run and every increasing target is in \(B\). It hit a new tie \(i\to t\) with \(\gamma_i>d_{it}\gamma_t\). This makes \(\delta_i+\log d_{it}<\delta_t\) in the old distances. Appending the new arc to an old shortest path strictly improves the distance to \(t\). The strict improvement transfers to the chosen new optimal flow by zero-cost cycle replacement.

For fixed seed \(b\) and reached vertex \(u\), every finite shortest distance is the cost of a simple path. The potential part is \(\phi(u)-\phi(b)\). Each remaining log label has a signed integer coefficient of magnitude at most \(m-1\), so each coordinate has at most \(Q\) possible distances. During a plateau of local cost, each coordinate can drop at most \(Q-1\) times. At least one drops on every transition, giving at most \(1+m(Q-1)\) inputs per plateau. Multiplying the counts for the seed, reached set, cost and distance plateaus, then adding failures, proves (1). L2 bounds the retained numerator and denominator lengths after \(j\) calls by \(O((j+1)\operatorname{poly}(L))\). Its rational surrogate costs select true logarithmic minimizers, its shortest-path products are compared exactly, and it returns rational vertices. Substituting (1) gives the claimed bit complexity. [L2, rational costs and denominator growth](L2-exact-arithmetic-audit.md#bit-length-bound-and-exact-lp-outputs)

In the six-state edge-revisit example of L1, exact enumeration of the feasible unit-scaled assignments and rational shortest-path products gives reached-set sizes \(3,5,7,7,7,7\). At the third and fourth states the exponential of four times the total cost is \(265236\), and the minimum path product to \(C\) falls from \(21/23\) to \(1/12\). At the fifth state that cost product drops to \(4278\), while the path product to \(B\) rises from \(5/2\) to \(155\). The final transition keeps the cost product at \(4278\) and decreases the path product to \(B\) to \(630/23\). This verifies both kinds of progress on the same recurring-edge instance. [L1, exact edge-revisit instance](L1-published-algorithm.md#exact-edge-revisit-instance)

## A multiplicative-rank extension for binary rational data

Orient viable edges from row to target. Choose a spanning forest of this graph and positive rational vertex potentials \(\pi(v)\), starting at 1 in each component, so that \(\pi(t)/\pi(i)=d_{it}\) on the forest. Put
\[
g_{it}=d_{it}\,\pi(i)/\pi(t).
\]
Tree labels are 1. Each other \(g_{it}\) is the signed product of the input slopes on its fundamental cycle. All alternating cycle products are products of these labels and their inverses. Let \(h\) be the multiplicative rank of the subgroup of positive rationals that they generate, namely the dimension of the rational span of their prime-exponent vectors. This definition is independent of the spanning forest. If the positive input slopes have numerators and denominators below \(2^b\), the absolute value of each prime exponent of each normalized edge label is at most \(mb\).

One can select \(h\) prime coordinates so that projection onto them is injective on this span, by choosing linearly independent columns in the matrix of exponent vectors. Every product of normalized labels is therefore determined by its \(h\) selected exponents. For a simple path these exponents are at most \(m^2b\) in magnitude. For a forest cost, group (3) by \(M_j\) to obtain \(\sum_j M_j\log G_j\), where \(G_j=\prod_e g_e^{c_{ej}}\). Each exponent of \(G_j\) is at most \(m^3b\) in magnitude. The preceding proof of (1) now applies with
\[
Q=(2m^2 b+1)^h,\qquad C=(2m^3 b+1)^{h(P+G)}. \tag{4}
\]
This gives polynomial bit time for fixed \(P,G,h\). The earlier \(D\)-label promise implies \(h\le D-1\), since in a cycle all vertex factors cancel and the exponent sum of the unreduced labels is zero. More generally, slopes that factor into row and column factors times arbitrary integer powers of a fixed number of positive generators have bounded cycle rank, even when the input does not supply exponents. The dimension-only iteration count in (1) uses the finite-label hypothesis. The broader count (4) can depend on the binary length \(b\).

The rank can also be computed in polynomial time without a prime factorization. Start with the numerator and denominator of each reduced \(g_{it}\) as lists of integer factors greater than 1, omitting units. Whenever two **distinct** factor values share a gcd greater than 1, split their occurrences by that gcd wherever the split is proper. Each round increases the total number of factor occurrences, which is at most the sum of the binary lengths of the original numerators and denominators. The resulting distinct bases are pairwise coprime and therefore multiplicatively independent. Rational elimination on the resulting integer exponents computes exactly \(h\). The continuation itself only uses the underlying rational slopes.

The three restrictions can be imposed separately on each connected component of the viable graph. The reached group lies entirely in its seed’s component, so in (2) it suffices to sum each component’s count of positive ratios, then add one for zero. The cost and distance bounds are the maximum of the corresponding local bounds. Thus different components may use disjoint sets of prior values, inverse gains and cycle generators. Another extension allows priors and inverse gains given as integer combinations of a bounded number of respective base masses with coefficients of magnitude at most \(K\). In (2) the coefficient bounds become \(kK,nK\) for the numerator and denominator. In (3) they become \(mK\) per edge, and hence \(m^2K\) for the finite-label cost count. A polynomial bound on \(K\) in \(L\) suffices.

## Zero slopes and gains

For literal raw slope classes one may replace all zeros by the same sufficiently small positive number and apply A1’s support repair. To preserve the general cycle-rank promise with arbitrary row and target factors, take the viable graph of the **original** weak instance and choose the forest potentials \(\pi\) on its positive-slope subgraph, including isolated vertices with potential 1. When using a per-component rank bound here, take the rank of the union of positive-edge cycle generators within each original viable component. Components joined only by zero-slope edges may contribute independent positive-edge cycles.

Take a positive rational tolerance \(\tau\) at most the determinant tolerance in A1 and strictly below every positive gap \(\max_s(a_{is}-d_{is})-a_{it}\) on an originally nonviable edge. If there are no such gaps, only the determinant bound is needed. Choose \(\epsilon=\tau/\max(1,\max_{i,t}\pi(t)/\pi(i))\). Replace every zero loss by \(\epsilon\pi(t)/\pi(i)\), and every zero defender gain by \(\tau\). These rational coefficients have polynomial bit length, and every change is at most \(\tau\). Each row’s full-coverage maximum falls by at most \(\tau\), so no formerly nonviable edge becomes viable. In the old potential normalization, previously positive viable labels belong to their original cycle group and the new labels all equal \(\epsilon\). Around a cycle the potentials still cancel, hence the rank rises by at most one. The number of positive inverse-gain classes rises by at most one. The strictly positive game satisfies the bounds above. Its equilibrium on its support and boundary pattern witnesses original violation at most \(\tau\), forcing the minimum violation in the original rational repair LP to equal zero by the determinant bound. Zero-prior rows and an explicit defender mixture are recovered as in A1. ## Relation to known results and the remaining obstruction

Arunachaleswaran, Barman and Rathi already count integer slope multiplicities to bound the distinct weights of perfect **unit** matchings, and explicitly obtain polynomial time for a constant number of slope values in their rent-division model. Their Theorem 5 computes minimum nonnegative envy-free prices when all slopes are powers of \(1+\epsilon\), in time polynomial in \(1/\epsilon\) and input size. The count above extends the weight argument to fractional forest flows with binary margins, including iterations at which the target totals outside the reached group are unrestricted. The Hall thresholds and monotone reachability connect that count to the defender’s prescribed resource total. The collected rank-one solver permits arbitrary priors and gains, and its fixed-type solver permits arbitrary slopes. The present result permits arbitrarily many interacting types and cycle products different from one. External priority for the security-game bound has not been established. [Arunachaleswaran, Barman and Rathi, §4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.04163#page=15) · [Theorem 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.04163#page=24) · [L3, prior partial results](L3-prior-partials.md)

In the assignment-market literature, Balkanski, En and Faenza claim a polynomial bound for an ascending auction. Algorithm 3, line 6 allows a new tie between an already reached buyer and an already reached item. Lemma 34 charges a non-breakpoint event to gaining an item. Oki and Sato identify this internal event in both that analysis and Dütting et al.’s earlier analysis. The local cost and distance plateaus above explicitly count such ties in the security-game continuation. [Balkanski, En and Faenza, Algorithm 3 and Lemma 34](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.13620#page=15) · [Oki and Sato, §6, footnote 13 and Appendix F](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10563v3#page=22)

The proof leaves two distinct counting obstacles in the general case. Priors \(p_i=2^{i-1}/(2^k-1)\), for \(1\le i\le k\), have \(2^k\) different subset sums. Even **at uniform margins**, path products can already take exponentially many values on one successful input. Use targets \(t_0,\ldots,t_s\), two rows in each layer \(j=1,\ldots,s\) tied only between \(t_{j-1},t_j\), and two further rows restricted to \(t_s\). Give every row the same prior and all targets the same gain. Put uncovered payoffs 0 on the indicated edges and \(-2\) elsewhere. Use loss 1 everywhere except for the forward destination edge of one row per layer, where the loss is the \(j\)-th distinct prime. Use budget one, initialize at zero coverage, and order targets by their indices so each row chooses the earlier target in its layer. Each target has two rows’ mass, so all are in \(B\). These margins force precisely this flow by induction from \(t_0\). From seed \(t_0\) there are \(2^s\) simple residual paths to \(t_s\) with pairwise different products, by choosing one of the two rows in each layer. Only their minimum is the current distance. B3e gives a separate family with a long sequence of actual distance changes. B3f forces a superpolynomial sequence of distinct flow costs even when all three margin quantities \(p,z,\lambda\) are fixed.
